Preface

A practical guide to getting work done on MedicineBow, the University of Wyoming’s high-performance computing (HPC) cluster, run by the Advanced Research Computing Center (ARCC).

It is written for people joining the ASCEND engine and its ARID program, and assumes you are comfortable on a computer but have never used a shared supercomputer. Each chapter explains why before how.

Why this book exists

The official documentation — the ARCC wiki — is good but has gaps and is in places out of date. The content here was written while onboarding on the live cluster and cross-checked against the running system in July 2026. Where the docs and the machine disagree, this book follows the machine and notes the conflict.

This is not an official ARCC document

It’s a community onboarding guide. It can drift out of date, and ARCC can change policy at any time. When in doubt, the official wiki and arcc-help@uwyo.edu are authoritative. Corrections are very welcome.

Quick orientation

  1. You cannot request your own account — your PI must. No project, no account. (See Getting an account.)
  2. Nothing on the cluster is backed up. Not home, not project, not scratch. There are snapshots, but they live on the same array. (See Where your data lives.)
  3. Never run real computation on a login node. Log in, then hand work to the scheduler (Slurm) or grab an interactive session. (See Why a scheduler exists.)
  4. Every job must name your project account with --account=<project>. (See Anatomy of a batch job.)
  5. The partition and QOS you choose decide how long you wait and how big you can go. Short test runs and long simulations belong in different queues (Partitions and QOS on MedicineBow, Queue times: which partition for what).

How the book is organized

The chapters run roughly in the order you’ll need them: getting an account and logging in, a minimal Linux survival kit, the filesystems and moving data, software (modules, conda, containers), then the bulk of the book on Slurm — asking for resources, picking a queue, watching jobs, arrays, GPUs, and handling preemption and failures. The last few chapters are quick reference: a cheat sheet, troubleshooting, and a glossary.

Conventions: shell commands you type are shown in code blocks. Boxes marked Gotcha or Important mark places people reliably get burned.
