6  Partitions, QOS, and queue times

Where to send a job and how long you’ll wait — the highest-leverage choices you make.

6.1 Partitions and QOS on MedicineBow

6.1.1 Partition = which hardware

A partition is a named pool of nodes. Submit with --partition=<name> (the default is mb).

Partition Nodes Cores/node Mem/node GPUs/node Good for
mb (default) 25 96 (EPYC 9454) ~1023 GB — general CPU work
mb-h100 6 96 ~1281 GB 8× H100 large model training, PINNs
mb-l40s 4 96 ~765 GB 8× L40S¹ inference, mid-size training
mb-a30 8 96 ~765 GB 8× A30 dev/GPU, smaller models
mb-a6000 1 64 ~1023 GB 4× A6000 GPU dev
mb-r6000 3 72 ~515 GB 4× R6000 GPU dev
debug 4 28 ~128 GB — quick tests (pair with debug QOS)
teton ~250 32 ~120 GB+ — large CPU throughput, arrays
beartooth 9 56 ~257 GB+ — CPU work
wildiris 5 48 ~507 GB+ — CPU work
non-investor 110 mixed mixed — CPU work you don’t want preempted
non-investor-gpu 7 mixed mixed mixed GPU work you don’t want preempted
inv-* varies varies varies varies investor-owned; borrowable but preemptible

¹ One L40S node has 4 GPUs instead of 8. Numbers are the machine as observed in July 2026; verify current state with showpartitions and sinfo.

The inv-* partitions are investor (“condo”) hardware: you can run on them when they’re idle, but the owner can reclaim the node and preempt your job (Section 8.1).

Avoiding preemption: use non-investor, not --constraint

--constraint=non-investor matches nothing on the current machine and does nothing. Use a partition instead: --partition=non-investor (110 nodes) or --partition=non-investor-gpu (7 GPU nodes; you must also request a GPU with --gres=gpu:N there) stays on hardware that won’t be reclaimed. This is the right home for long, un-checkpointed runs.

You can list several partitions and let Slurm use whichever frees up first; --partition=mb,teton,beartooth is common for CPU throughput work.

6.1.2 QOS = which service tier

A Quality of Service sets three things at once: your maximum walltime, your CPU footprint cap, and your priority. Submit with --qos=<name> (the default is normal).

QOS Priority Max walltime CPU cap / account Your project can use it?
interactive 90 8 h 4746 ✅
fast 80 12 h 4746 ✅
normal (default) 70 3 d 4746 ✅
long 60 7 d 2876 ✅
debug 0 1 h 7 cpu / 32 G / 2 nodes ✅
extended 50 14 d 2157 ❌ (not granted)
inv-* (investor) 110 14 d varies ❌ (owners only)

Confirm your own list any time:

sacctmgr -n show assoc user=$USER format=qos
# <project>:  debug, fast, interactive, long, normal
sqos                         # full QOS table with limits
The walltime trade-off

Longer requested walltime means lower priority and a smaller footprint cap. fast (12 h) gives priority 80 and the full 4746-core cap; long (7 d) drops to priority 60 and a ~40%-smaller cap. This trade-off drives the recommendations in Section 6.2.

6.1.3 DenyOnLimit: rejected at submit

Every user-facing QOS is flagged DenyOnLimit. If you ask for more than a QOS allows — say a 5-day run under normal (max 3 d), or more cores than the cap — Slurm rejects the job at submit time with a clear error rather than queuing it indefinitely. Fix the request and resubmit.

6.1.4 Putting both knobs in a script

#SBATCH --partition=mb          # hardware: general CPU nodes
#SBATCH --qos=fast              # tier: 12h max, high priority
#SBATCH --time=02:00:00         # within the fast 12h ceiling

For a GPU job:

#SBATCH --partition=mb-l40s
#SBATCH --qos=normal
#SBATCH --gres=gpu:1            # ask for 1 GPU (see @sec-gpu)
#SBATCH --time=08:00:00

6.2 Queue times: which partition for what

6.2.1 What the queue looks like

At a representative midday moment on MedicineBow there were roughly 2,000 jobs running and 800 pending. Almost every pending job’s reason was Priority — the machine is busy and you’re in line, not that you asked for something impossible.

6.2.2 Measured wait times (start minus eligible)

The table below is from ~1.7 million jobs over a recent 3-day window, measured as the time between a job becoming eligible and starting. Read it for order of magnitude, not as a promise; your wait depends on how busy the machine is and how big your request is.

Partition Median wait 90th percentile Character
teton ~0 s ~13 min huge pool; throughput/array work starts fast
mb (default CPU) <1 min ~10 min plentiful; short CPU jobs start quickly
beartooth ~4 min ~11 min steady CPU capacity
mb-a30 (GPU) ~2 min ~1.5 h smaller GPUs, usually gettable
mb-h100 (GPU) seconds–hours ~8–9 h bimodal: idle now, or a long wait
mb-l40s (GPU) ~1 h ~2–6 h consistently contended
beartooth-gpu ~1 h ~7 h contended

By QOS, the CPU signal is even clearer:

QOS Median wait 90th percentile
interactive ~11 s ~30 s
fast ~0 s ~13 min
normal ~19 s ~8 min
How to read these numbers

This window included a large fast-QOS job array on teton, which pulls the teton/fast medians toward zero. The normal-QOS row (tens of thousands of ordinary jobs: median ~19 s, 90th percentile ~8 min) is the most representative figure for a normal CPU job. The pattern holds either way: CPU work starts fast; GPU work, especially L40S/H100, is where you wait. Measure the live picture before a big campaign — see the commands at the end of this chapter.

6.2.3 Short proof-of-concept vs. long simulation

6.2.3.1 Short proof-of-concept / debugging / “does it even run?”

You want to start now, run briefly, and iterate. Priority and low wait matter; walltime and footprint don’t.

#SBATCH --partition=debug        # 4 dedicated test nodes, usually idle
#SBATCH --qos=debug              # 1 h max, but starts almost instantly
#SBATCH --time=00:30:00
#SBATCH --cpus-per-task=2
#SBATCH --mem=8G
  • Use the debug partition + debug QOS for the fastest possible start on a tiny job (cap: 1 h, ≤2 nodes, ≤7 cores, ≤32 G).
  • Need a bit more than debug allows but still want to start fast? Use fast QOS on mb (12 h ceiling, priority 80) — median start of seconds.
  • Developing interactively? interactive QOS starts in ~10–30 s (Section 7.3).
  • For GPU proof-of-concept, develop on mb-a30 (starts in minutes), not H100.

6.2.3.2 Long production simulation / big ensemble / final runs

You need days of walltime and want to avoid preemption, accepting a longer initial wait in exchange for a long, stable run.

#SBATCH --partition=non-investor   # big pool that won't be reclaimed
#SBATCH --qos=long                 # up to 7 days
#SBATCH --time=5-00:00:00
#SBATCH --cpus-per-task=16
#SBATCH --mem=32G
  • Use long QOS for multi-day runs (7 d ceiling). normal (3 d) is the default and fine for sub-3-day jobs.
  • Submit to --partition=non-investor (CPU) or non-investor-gpu (GPU) so an investor can’t reclaim your node mid-run. Or list several CPU partitions (--partition=mb,teton,beartooth) to start on whichever frees first — at the cost of possible preemption on inv-* hardware.
  • Checkpoint (Section 8.1.3) so a node failure, walltime overrun, or preemption doesn’t cost you the whole run.
  • Right-size first (Section 5.3): a leaner request under long starts sooner than a bloated one.

6.2.3.3 The quick decision table

Your situation Partition QOS Why
“Does my script run at all?” debug debug near-instant start, 1 h cap
Fast CPU test, up to 12 h mb fast priority 80, full core cap, seconds to start
Interactive development mb interactive ~10–30 s start, live shell
Normal CPU job, < 3 days mb (or mb,teton) normal default; ~20 s median start
Long CPU simulation, 3–7 days non-investor long big non-preemptible pool
GPU proof-of-concept mb-a30 normal GPUs you can usually get in minutes
GPU production / big training mb-h100 / mb-l40s normal/long most power; expect an hours-long wait

6.2.4 Check the live queue before you commit

Historical medians are a guide; the live machine is what matters. Before a big submission:

showpartitions               # idle vs busy nodes, per partition, right now
sinfo -p mb-h100 -o "%P %t %D"   # how many H100 nodes are idle this second?
gpuavail                     # ARCC helper: free GPUs by type
squeue -p mb-l40s            # how deep is the line for this partition?
showuserlimits               # what am I allowed to ask for?

If showpartitions shows idle H100 nodes, submit now. If the GPU partitions are full and the line is long, either develop on mb-a30 or submit and walk away.

Once a job is queued, showjobreasons <jobid> explains why it’s still pending (Section 7.1) — usually Priority (wait) or Resources (large request).

Section 6.3 explains why QOS dominates your position in that line.

6.3 How priority is computed

6.3.1 The formula

Slurm computes a priority score for every pending job and runs the highest scores first (subject to backfill, below). The score is a weighted sum; MedicineBow’s weights are:

PriorityWeightQOS       = 200     <- dominant
PriorityWeightFairShare = 100
PriorityWeightJobSize   = 100
PriorityWeightAge       = 10
PriorityWeightPartition = 0       (partition doesn't affect priority here)

Each factor is a number between 0 and 1, multiplied by its weight, and summed. The biggest lever, by a wide margin, is QOS.

6.3.2 What each factor means

QOS (weight 200). Higher-priority QOS means a better position. From Section 6.1: interactive (90) > fast (80) > normal (70) > long (60). Choosing fast over long is worth more priority than any amount of waiting, which is why the short-vs-long decision in Section 6.2 matters.

Fair-share (weight 100) — recent usage. Slurm tracks how much your account has used lately relative to its share. Heavy use this week lowers your fair-share factor, and thus your priority, for a while; it recovers as usage decays (half-life ~7 days here). This mechanism stops any one group from monopolizing the machine and rewards steady use over bursts.

Job size (weight 100). A factor based on the resources requested. With the SMALL_RELATIVE_TO_TIME flag this cluster sets, it interacts with your time request — another reason not to over-ask (Section 5.3).

Age (weight 10) — time spent waiting. A job gains priority the longer it sits, maxing out after ~3 days. At weight 10, age is a tie-breaker, not a strategy; you won’t age past someone who chose a better QOS.

6.3.3 Practical takeaway

QOS dominates priority

Because QOS is weighted 2× fair-share and 20× age, the highest-leverage choice for start time is the most appropriate high-priority QOS your job fits — the shortest walltime tier that still lets it finish (Section 6.2). Waiting longer, resubmitting, or submitting at 3am have little effect by comparison.

6.3.4 Backfill: why a small job can jump the line

Priority sets the order, but Slurm also backfills: if the top job is waiting for, say, 40 nodes to free up, Slurm slots a smaller, shorter job into the gap now, as long as that doesn’t delay the top job’s reserved start time. This is why a 20-minute test can start immediately even when the queue looks deep, and why short jobs start sooner than long ones. Requesting less time and fewer resources makes your job backfill-eligible in more gaps — another payoff for right-sizing.

6.3.5 Seeing your own priority

sprio -j <jobid>            # the priority breakdown for a pending job
sprio -l                    # all pending jobs, with factor columns
showjobreasons <jobid>      # ARCC helper: plain-language "why am I waiting?"

If sprio shows a low QOS factor because you’re on long but the job would finish in 10 hours, resubmitting under fast is the most effective change you can make.
